    Clover and Moose

    The simulated dog that runs at the robot has a name now. Two, actually: Clover and Moose, the two dogs who will share the real lawn with this robot. Clover got a sim model last week. Moose asked (through Chris) not to be left out, so he got one too. Making his turned up a bug that had been quietly spoiling the camera tests, and filming them both showed us something about where a mower's camera can and can't look.

    Which way is it facing?

    The last post ended with one false alarm left: a fence that "moved" because the robot thought it was facing 7° away from where it really was. 7° doesn't sound like much. At 5 m it moves a fence 60 cm, and that's enough to look like something walking. This post fixes the heading, and on the way found that the simulator had been flattering us.

    Things that move

    A post in the lawn is an obstacle: stop, go round it. A child is not, even standing still: stop, blade off, and wait until they've gone. The camera will one day say "that's a child". Until then the lidar can still tell one thing apart: something that moves. This post teaches the robot to see that, and to treat anything that moves as a person.

    Is the lidar telling the truth?

    Every safety check so far trusts the lidar: it stops for the dog, the drop and the steep bank because the lidar saw them. This project's first rule says the lidar is the sensor we believe. So we have to know when it's lying: a muddy window, water on the lens, rain, a driver that hangs. This post adds that check, and breaks the simulated lidar five ways to test it.

    The edge of the terrace

    A mower can hurt itself, or someone near it, without hitting anything. The worst way is driving off an edge: a retaining wall, a step, a pond bank. A fall can't be undone. This post gives the robot two separate ways to stay on top: the map, and its own eyes.

    The last post taught the robot to stop its blade before a garden path, as long as the path was on the map. If a path isn't drawn, or is drawn in the wrong place, the robot would mow straight over it. This post adds the second layer: the robot sees the paving for itself.

    Blade off on the paving

    A spinning blade on gravel or paving throws stones. Until now the robot avoided the problem by never leaving the lawn, so it couldn't cross a garden path, a patio or a driveway at all. That's the first of the three ground hazards in the perception plan (5.1c). The other two are drop-offs and slopes.
